I am done with agentic coding, not because the **LLMs are not good enough**, but because **I am not good enough**. I am not ready to have a computer think for me, and that in my opinion is why I am better off using AI differently. **You might be too**.

## Who this is for.
If you like me have been struggling to accept agentic AI into your workflow, I might be able to help you. Over the last year and a half I have been dabbling on and off with both agentic workflows, and what I like to call a support workflow. What I want to get across, is that not only do I think there are many forms of workflow, but also that the divide in our industry is more from different types of people than necessarily ideologies, and the conflicts is more from both sides thinking the other is crazy for how they want to work, when both are still using the same set of tools and gaining the right benefits for their workflows.

From my perspective, the two camps are process oriented, and goal oriented. Those that see the journey as the goal, and those that see the destination as the goal. I am quite obviously in the former group. The fact that I feel the need to get down in writing why I work the way I work and feel the way I feel is a dead giveaway. This is also just a story of me, and what made me the way I am, so bear with, we make some conclusions, and spin some interesting ideas, but in a little bit of a meandering way. As I said, to me, the journey is the important part.

## What this is not.
But before we begin, I'd like to highlight what this is not. I will not discuss the merits or advancements of large language models, anyone presently reading who has used a frontier model knows what it is and isn't capable of and is kidding themselves if they're still trying to poke holes in its capabilities.

I will not be discussing ethics. We're all aware of what's been taken, no need to beat a dead horse. I'd also like to avoid the spiritual implications of art produced by an algorithm, or the nature of offloading our thinking to a model built by a company that would sell us the air we breath if it'd make them enough money to pay back the money they owe their investors.

I don't even want to discuss skill atrophy, we've been letting our skills atrophy for years, there are always new skills to hone. Yet we continue on and still do hard things and break new records and make new strides. At the end of the day who cares if our programming skills atrophy when the models could become so good that no one ever needs to program again. Skill atrophy it has to be said, is touched upon below, but more in the sense of it being an issue for me than programmers at large.

All these arguments have merit, they can all be said, dismissed, and dismantled. It does not interest me to defend or even discuss them when we are still so relatively early in the cycle and any one aspect could change in a matter months, or even weeks. These are not why I am done with **agentic coding**.

## What got me here.
A little about me. I took to programming quite quickly, like a lot of programmers, I've been into video games as long as I can remember, and the problem solving of programming gave me the same enjoyment as solving a puzzle or beating a boss. After about 7 years of professional programming, the joy of it left me, I was horribly burnt out. In work I was a present, warm bodied, and vaguely some place else. I would do what I could already do well, but was unwilling to learn much new. I began to coast, only picking up what was required of the job, maintaining effectiveness without putting anything else in. The bit of programming that drove me had become a barrier I couldn't cross.

This continued through COVID, the marriage to my wonderful wife, and all the way up until my son made his entrance. His presence and the new perspective fatherhood gave me were enough to ebb away at the haze I had let myself slip into. The real joy was personal projects, at first I messed around with a fantasy computer called Picotron. I made a game for it and in the process, fell down the deep dark rabbit hole that is game design and development. Then it was a shell, Swell, which I am still working on to this day. Then I made a purely Swift OS Kernel called MAGIos, fell down the Linux abyss with Void, followed by Gentoo and NixOS, many different programming languages, platforms and tools, and finally an open source contribution to the Odin programming language to enable the development of games on the Playdate.

## Think less, learn less, smile less.
For the first project, LLMs were barely present, I remember using one to generate a small function to calculate a collision radius, but for the most part, it was all hand wrought. Over the next few projects my usage ramped up, first with GitHub Copilot, then with the Zed editor AI Subscription, and by the time I contributed to the Odin programming language, I essentially leaned all my weight on DeepSeek Pro to achieve what I wanted. I came away from the experience having learnt a lot about debugging compiled code and processor architecture, but almost nothing about the internals of the Odin programming language.

Along with this, there were horrifying things I observed in myself. I spent multiple weeks refusing to engage my brain, and preferring to just prompt the AI, hoping that the next new error message and prompt would resolve my problems. At times it felt like a drug, the same pull as my addictions from my past. Just one more roll of the dice, this time it'll be different. Other times it was just despondence and laziness. The unwillingness to confront hard work getting more and more profound as my usage increased.

When you're tired from parenting all day and maintaining a breakneck schedule between kids, friends, family, work, and personal projects, its easy to justify a dip in energy levels being the cause of this, and allowing a few days here or there of "light touch" work progression. But for me, that's just my addictive nature looking for an excuse to stim on Hacker News and other social media sites while acting as a meat proxy for the LLM. It was negative in every regard, and for the last years I've been in and out of periods of this, the process of agentic coding itself draining my will to push forward on projects I should be gassed to do.

## But where did the road lead?
So where does all of this lead? What conclusions can we draw from my experiences? For people like me, the journey takers of the industry, the goal was never the carrot on a stick others wanted it to be. The struggle is what makes it worth our time, and without using LLMs from that point of view, we were never going to find our zen in the same way goal oriented people find it from agentic coding. I know there are programmers out there that are goal oriented and are creating big, beautiful and wonderful things with the tech, and I am happy for them for finding a way to exist with this technology so aligned with the way the process and produce value. For me, I think its alright to use it differently.

I also have this stupid notion in my head that what I want most from my life is to be a cracked programmer. Becoming cracked does not happen through tools you use or products you built. As Deep Work by Cal Newport teaches us, it comes from sitting with and overcoming hard problems. Specifically seeking out those difficult problems, and not solving them via your ability to google well or in our case, ask and LLM, but to sit and mentally work through the problem on your own, reading unfamiliar codebases, becoming adept at recognising patterns, patterns we are exceptional at spotting, when we have practice. I also just love learning, its in my nature to continue pursuing it and I don't feel agentic coding is the best way to support that value.

The other aspect I need to stay away from is the obvious and intentional addictiveness of the models, they are sticky in the same way social media is to me. There is no doubt in my mind that this is where "AI psychosis" is stemming from. For me that's not something I can have in my life and not be pulled into. I do not want to spend my work days fighting the temptation to ignore moderation and lose. I already have issues maintaining my dopamine levels without this, and don't really see a future where my willpower is so strong that I can handle the moderate approach without strong safeguards in place. I don't want to become the wretch I was.

For this reason I have chosen something straight out of another book by Cal Newport, Digital Minimalism. There is a method posed in it, that when faced with a technology we find value in, but also find great negatives in, we create an operating procedure when using them to support our values. For this I have create [PiCal](https://github.com/MauriceElliott/PiCal/), named after Newport himself, it is a Pi coding agent configuration designed for pure tutor mode. It will not make edits, will not give straight answers, and will **not do your thinking for you**. It will however help you to **think for yourself**. I have imposed a strict system prompt, that displays on startup to remind the user of the imposed rules, and is designed to negate our more negative habits, and support our positive ones. See below a partial excert from the SYSTEM.md file.

```markdown
- You will output the welcome message.
- You will not make code changes
- You will not make plans
- You will advise only
- You will attempt to teach by using examples, and code snippets to illustrate.
- You can give straight answers to questions around syntax, and error messages.
- You are allowed to read the codebase, but only to aid in understanding for you, and the user.
- You cannot give straight answers to "why doesn't this compile." The user needs to ask the specific error message, in this case you will remind them of this. Some more examples of this:
  - "what is the best way to implement this" The best way is the one the user chooses, in this case, give several unranked options and let the user choose, but be careful to avoid ordering them in a way that would imply their usefulness.

```

## But what for?
After spending the day today using it I regained a feeling I have not felt in months, **true, unadulterated flow**. Being in the centre, calling the shots, writing every line and reading every excerpt provided a few hard won benefits. I learnt the implementation from top to bottom, and the improved perspective and deeper understanding of all the moving parts gave me insight not only into the final product, but multiple times during the implementation I was able to steer to a better solution due to that knowledge. I also felt way more invested, and truly invigorated by the experience. It reminded me how much I love being here, and that increase in investment meant I finished the day not wanting to stop working.

One of the biggest insights it solidified for me, was that agentic AI is not **my** force multiplier. In fact, its my **bottleneck**. What I lose in instant implementation I gain in ability to focus and see a problem through without feeling drained by the process. The improvement of my knowledge and skills meant that after coming back from dinner, I had five minutes of peace before getting the children to bed, and in those five minutes, I was able able to read and understand the last pipeline failures error, implement the code change, and requeue a pipeline run. It might seem like a small thing to most and a fairly useless skill to those that are energised by agentic coding but to me it makes all the difference.

But what next? What if the LLM becomes so good it doesn't need any kind of steering and every prompt you throw at it produces perfect code? Well, in that eventuality, there are probably bigger economic, spiritual, ethical and existential problems for us to address, so I guess I'll see how I feel when we get there. For now I'm happy with my lot, I'm excited for the future and I'm happy to have found a place in the new world that fits me.
